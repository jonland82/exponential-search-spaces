The question. A search over \(n\) binary choices has \(2^n\) candidate states. Can exact summaries or certified exclusions account for enough of them that the remaining search is small? Can we obtain those summaries cheaply enough to help?

The narrative arc

Reading order: I: Framework → II: Exact-search test → III: Random-target probability → IV: Pairing in approximation. Notes III and IV branch from II; either can be read independently after the framework and exact-search test.

Continue with V: Structural distance (working draft) and VI: Learned completion and certified pruning. These return to exact solving: discover easy cores, predict budgeted completion, then test whether structural checks improve search within Note I's framework. VII: Geometric core reuse (working draft) develops an exact route that improves paid completion on fresh constructed inputs.

Two short companions explain why unresolved inputs cluster and when the geometric runtime bound improves on general search. From Choices to Sums connects the series to OpenAI's subset-sum algorithms. Notes V, VII and the runtime companion retain their working-draft labels.

Note III · Random-target probability

Probability-Valued Subset Sum: Certified Bounds and Learned Guidance

Draw a target by a specified rule and ask how likely it is to be reachable. Range and collision certificates provably tighten the probability bounds. A fresh multi-size benchmark finds modest range tightening and little extra benefit from the tested collision refinements. Calibrated learning narrows much more on matched data, but fails size transfer.

\[ \mu(L)\le q_\mu(w)\le\mu(U). \]

\(q_\mu(w)\) is reachability probability for weights \(w\) under target distribution \(\mu\). Witnessed targets \(L\) and a sound superset \(U\) bound it.

Note IV · A controlled negative result

Does Pairing Add Value to Certified Approximation? A Subset-Sum Experiment

If we only need to get close to the target, and know how close, can our framework avoid much of the exponential search? Approximation supplies a precise accuracy guarantee. The controlled experiment asks what pairing adds: it finds no consistent additional timing benefit over the same additive rule used sequentially in the key comparison, with one additional input completed within the state cap.

\[ \begin{gathered} \ell\le d^*(w,t)\le u,\\ u-\ell\le\eta. \end{gathered} \]

\(\ell,u\) bound the best possible distance \(d^*(w,t)\) from a subset sum of weights \(w\) to target \(t\); \(\eta\) is the allowed gap.

Note V · Structural distance · Working draft

How Far from Easy? Structural Distance in Subset Sum

Finds a largest superincreasing core and searches only its exceptions. Logarithmically many exceptions give a sufficient polynomial regime. More elaborate core selectors reduce residual support on some constructed lists, but their discovery costs outweigh the query savings in the experiment.

\[ T(x)\le F(L)+2^k\bigl(Q(L)+\operatorname{poly}(L)\bigr). \]

\(k\) is the number of exceptions; \(F\) discovers the core and \(Q\) answers an exact core query. Working draft, available in HTML/PDF with source.

The key formalism

In Note I, \(n\) is the number of binary choices and \(C(n)\) counts states handled by exact representation or certified exclusion. The residual \(r(n)\) must still be searched explicitly. Let \(T(n)\) be the modeled runtime; coverage overhead includes the work of discovering, representing, and certifying covered states:

\[ r(n)=2^n-C(n),\qquad T(n)=\text{coverage overhead}+r(n). \]

Within this model, polynomial overhead leads to polynomial runtime exactly when the residual is polynomially bounded. Covering a large fixed percentage of \(2^n\) states is not enough.

Note III changes the outcome being measured. Fix a list of weights \(w=(w_1,\ldots,w_n)\), and let \(A(w)\) be its set of attainable subset sums. Draw a target \(T\) from a specified distribution \(\mu\). Let \(L\) contain witnessed reachable targets and \(U\) every target not soundly excluded. For any target set \(B\), let \(\mu(B)\) mean the probability that \(T\in B\):

\[ q_\mu(w)=\Pr_{T\sim\mu}\!\bigl[T\in A(w)\bigr], \qquad \mu(L)\le q_\mu(w)\le\mu(U). \]

The bounds hold for a particular weight list; their width measures how much remains unresolved about the random-target question.

Note IV fixes the target and measures the minimum attainable distance instead. An actual subset supplies the upper bound; an error-aware summary supplies a lower bound:

\[ d^*(w,t)=\min_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}\left|\sum_i x_iw_i-t\right|, \qquad \ell\le d^*(w,t)\le u,\quad u-\ell\le\eta. \]

The tolerance \(\eta\) permits nearby partial sums to share a representative while retaining a deterministic guarantee. The experiments compare the total work needed to reach the same tolerance.

Note VII reuses one exact geometric core table across exceptional assignments. The runtime companion gives a conservative bit-cost bound:

\[ T_{\mathrm{route}}=F+O^*(HW+2^kGW). \]

Here \(F\) pays discovery, \(k\) counts exceptions, \(H\times W\) is the core table, and \(G\le H\) bounds candidate queries per assignment. The notation \(O^*\) suppresses polynomial factors in binary input length. Polynomial discovery and table size, with logarithmically many exceptions, suffice for polynomial time on that structured family; no such guarantee holds for arbitrary inputs.

What the results say

Exact core reuse improves paid completion on fresh constructed inputs. Note VII's original route gains 16 completions over matched root geometry, with a whole-list-bootstrap 95% interval approximately [4,30]. Its later bounds-first refinement avoids 90 of 182 tables, but the primary incremental completion difference is only +1 with interval [0,3], and no clear additional wall-clock gain. These comparisons support a scoped practical improvement.

Easy structure can certify whole branches. Note VI's follow-up implements Note I's proposal and verification framework. Structural checks improve the observed number of exact completions over DFS, but the transferred ML gate does not beat a simple distance rule. The paired uncertainty intervals span zero; these are results on a constructed test set.

Better structure must pay for discovery. Note V's selectors reduce residual support on 18 of 54 structured lists, yet neither is faster on their 162 targets. Note VI likewise finds that fewer search nodes can coexist with more computation spent acquiring features.

Representation must earn its cost. The paired subset-sum solver was slower than meet-in-the-middle on all 18 constructed hard no-solution cases tested.

Prediction helps choose work. The classifier's held-out area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), a ranking measure, was 0.866, close to a simple density model's 0.864. Two of its 43 most confident NO proposals were actually YES, so exact elimination still needs a verifier.

Probability bounds can be tightened with certificates. On 160 fresh lists of 12–22 weights, range counting removes 14.15% of interval width on average, and tested collision/residue refinements raise this to 14.19%; every deterministic interval contains the truth. Separately, calibrated learning removes 76.96% on matched data with 97.5% coverage, but coverage falls to 88.75% on larger lists. Exact bitset computation often finishes faster.

The negative finding identifies what earns credit. With the common greedy search disabled, 32 items, and tolerance 0.01% of total weight, pairing is faster on 7 of 15 inputs that both it and sequential additive trimming certify. The median sequential/paired time ratio is 0.993. Pairing certifies all 16 inputs within the per-summary state cap; the sequential method certifies 15. This is a scoped negative timing result, with a resource-limit advantage retained.
