Testing your bound-tightening method on Note III

It worked on matched synthetic data. The main model removed 77.0% of the original interval width and covered 975 of 1000 exact answers. But its coverage fell to 88.8% on larger lists without recalibration.

The simple density baseline nearly matched the main model on the primary test and transferred better to larger lists. This supports calibrated refinement on this generator, with modest additional benefit from the richer structural model. It does not establish deterministic bounds for individual new lists.

What was frozen before the test

Using the repository’s weight generator and Note III’s target rule, generate 1,200 training lists, 500 calibration lists, and 1,000 untouched test lists, all with 16 weights. Each list independently belongs to the independent-weight or repeated-pair family with equal probability. Generate another 240 lists with 20 weights as a prespecified size-transfer stress test. The same fixed random seed, 731029, controls generation. No hyperparameter search, model selection, recalibration, or rule changes followed inspection of test results.

Targets are uniform on the integer middle 40% of each list’s total weight, conditional on passing reachable-residue screens modulo 8, 10, 16, and 32. The denominator is counted exactly via a period-160 residue calculation. All attainable sums automatically pass those screens.

The original lower endpoint is the fraction of eligible targets witnessed by 512 sampled subsets. The upper endpoint is the smaller of 1 and the number of multiplicity-count patterns divided by eligible-target count. Exact reachable fractions were computed by set enumeration for labels and evaluation only; they were excluded from test features and interval construction.

The primary predictor is a fixed gradient-boosted regressor of normalized position inside the original interval. It uses inexpensive list features: density, multiplicity, weight moments, small-block collisions, residue coverage, and original endpoints. The density baseline uses degree-two ridge regression on only density proxy, unique-weight fraction, and the original upper endpoint. The midpoint baseline predicts normalized position 1/2. All three receive the same one-sided normalized calibration procedure.

The theorem checked numerically

Let ℓ ≤ q ≤ u, W = u − ℓ, and q̂ = ℓ + pW, with p in [0,1]. On 500 calibration lists, the two one-sided scores are (q̂ − q)₊/W and (q − q̂)₊/W. Collapsed intervals contribute zero scores. For total error α = 0.05, the correction in each tail is ordered score number ceil((500 + 1) × 0.975) = 489. Under exchangeability, a union bound gives at least 95% marginal coverage.

I* = [max(ℓ, q̂ − c⁻W), min(u, q̂ + c⁺W)]
width(I*) / W = min(p, c⁻) + min(1 − p, c⁺)
fraction removed ≥ max(0, 1 − c⁻ − c⁺)

The structural-model corrections were c⁻ = 0.132124 and c⁺ = 0.099358. Thus every noncollapsed interval, including the shifted-size cases, removed at least 76.9% of its original width. The exact width identity, nonexpansion, and minimum-shrinkage inequality were checked for all 3,720 model/cohort predictions. Statistical containment does not follow from narrowness alone.

Selection diagnostic

The prespecified diagnostic retained the top 20% by predicted fractional shrinkage, using no test labels for selection. Structural-model coverage there was 197/200 (98.5%) on matched lists and 43/48 (89.6%) on larger lists. This particular selection did not create an additional observed collapse. It does not establish coverage conditional on arbitrary score-based selection; that remains subject to the issue in your selection paper.

Checks and cost

All 2,940 original certified intervals contained their exact reachable fraction. No weight list was duplicated across any cohorts. Set-enumeration labels were checked against brute force on 30 small inputs, and 12 primary-test labels were independently checked using integer-bitset dynamic programming. Every check passed.

The run took 55.9 seconds. Exact labels for training and calibration took 10.08 seconds in total; fitting the structural model took 0.24 seconds. On 1,000 primary-test lists, outer-bound construction, feature extraction, and batched structural inference averaged 1.51 ms per list. Exact enumeration averaged 6.10 ms per primary-test list. These compare different outputs: a statistical interval and the exact fraction. They are small Python benchmarks, not a claim of superiority over optimized bitset algorithms or a new complexity bound. Training-label costs are additional.

Verdict

The method is applicable and produced useful tightening here. Most of the benefit was also available to a simple density model. The richer model’s smaller matched-data intervals came with a clear size-transfer failure. A next prospective study should freeze a multi-size training and calibration population and compare cost to reach a specified interval width against direct exact computation and exact-label sampling.
