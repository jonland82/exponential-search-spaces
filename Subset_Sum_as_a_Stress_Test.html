Companion note II of III

Subset Sum as a Stress Test for Certified Coverage

Jonathan R. Landers
October 2026

Abstract. The first note gives a costed model of certified coverage in a \(2^n\)-state search space. Here subset sum supplies a concrete test: exact half-sum summaries, sound interval and residue exclusions, and learned proposals can all be implemented. On 18 constructed no-solution cases that pass cheap root checks, the tested paired solver is slower than meet-in-the-middle in every case. A probability classifier ranks held-out instances well, but its most confident NO proposals still contain YES cases. The experiments identify where this implementation’s costs lie; they do not establish a new exact algorithm. The third note asks a different, probability-valued question about reachable targets.

A concrete search space

Let \(w=(w_1,\ldots,w_n)\) be a list of \(n\) positive integer weights and \(t\) an integer target. Each binary choice \(x_i\) includes \(w_i\) when it is \(1\) and omits it when it is \(0\). Define \[Y(w,t)=\mathbf{1}\!\left\{\exists x\in\{0,1\}^n:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iw_i=t\right\}.\] Here \(\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}\) is \(1\) when its condition holds and \(0\) otherwise. The \(2^n\) vectors \(x\) are the direct-search states. If disjoint index sets \(A\) and \(B\) partition \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\), let \[\Sigma(A)=\left\{\sum_{i\in A}x_iw_i:x_i\in\{0,1\}\right\}, \qquad \Sigma(A\cup B)=\Sigma(A)+\Sigma(B),\] where \(+\) denotes the set of pairwise sums. Thus \(Y(w,t)=1\) exactly when some \(a\in\Sigma(A)\) has \(t-a\in\Sigma(B)\). Distinct subsets with the same sum share one exact summary value.

The tested paired search stores distinct sums for a sorted half of the weights and explores the other half with exact interval and residue checks. Meet-in-the-middle (MITM) constructs both half-sum sets and checks complements directly. A bounded depth-first search (DFS) provides a simpler pruning baseline. The algorithms answer the same decision question; the issue is whether the extra pairing and certificates cost less than the work they avoid.

Experiments and controls

An initial set of five constructed families compared bitset dynamic programming, MITM, bounded DFS, and the paired search at several sizes. All applicable exact implementations agreed with exhaustive search on 120 small cases. Narrow weights favored bitset dynamic programming. A mixed planted-solution family made pairing look fast against MITM, but bounded DFS was faster still. A modular no-solution family was rejected at the root by a residue-10 check; that simple arithmetic certificate could also be given to a stronger baseline. The complete timings are in the repository’s subset-sum/RESULTS.md.

The follow-up deliberately removes that easy explanation. We generated no-solution targets near half the total of random 41-bit odd weights, with each distinct value occurring once, twice, or three times. Sizes were \(n\in\{24,30,36\}\), with two seeds for each family-size combination. Every target passed whole-instance bounds and attainable-residue tests modulo \(8,10,16,32\), and every list had greatest common divisor (gcd) \(1\). The same cheap root checks were charged to all algorithms. Small validation cases agreed with exhaustive search.

Learned proposals

To test the learned-proposal part of the first note, a random forest was trained to predict \(Y(w,t)\) from summary features of a weight list and target. Labels were computed exactly by MITM. The data comprised independent-weight and repeated-pair families at \(n=16,18,20,22\); four targets were drawn per list from the middle \(40\%\) of its total sum, conditional on passing the cheap modular checks. Training, calibration, and test used disjoint lists: \(840\), \(180\), and \(180\), respectively. Another \(240\) lists at \(n=24\) tested an unseen size.

Let \(\widehat p(w,t)\) be the forest’s predicted chance that \(Y(w,t)=1\), abbreviated \(\widehat p\) when the input is understood. On the \(720\) held-out targets, its receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC AUC), a ranking measure, was \(0.866\); its Brier score, the mean squared difference between predicted chances and exact \(0/1\) labels, was \(0.133\); and accuracy was \(80.8\%\). A simple density-based logistic model had AUC \(0.864\) and Brier score \(0.136\). The forest ranked cases, but it did not reveal a clear advantage over density. Of \(43\) targets assigned \(\widehat p(w,t)\le 1\%\), \(2\) were actually YES.

Interpretation

The first note’s coverage criterion counts only soundly eliminated or exactly represented states and charges for finding those representations. Here exact half-sum representation is easy to state but expensive to construct. The tested residue certificates were either decisive at the root on an easy family or nearly inactive after easy cases were excluded. Learned scores can order exact checks; they cannot delete a predicted NO without a sound verifier.

This negative result is useful scope information. It motivates the third note’s narrower objective: for a specified random target, bound the fraction of targets that are reachable. That probability question admits exact upper and lower bounds even when one target’s decision answer remains binary.
